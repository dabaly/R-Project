PRESENTATION GUIDE
Analysis of the Relationship Between Rainfall, Temperature and Maize Yield in Kenya
Phase 1 and Phase 2 | Two-speaker guide

PRESENTATION PURPOSE
Explain how the group moved from a research idea (Phase 1) to collected, cleaned, explored, and interpreted data (Phase 2). The main message is that the 2010–2024 national data show weak negative associations between annual rainfall and maize yield, and between annual mean temperature and maize yield. The small dataset can show associations; it cannot prove that climate caused yield changes.

SUGGESTED PRESENTATION FLOW

SPEAKER 1 — PROJECT IDEA AND PHASE 1

1. Opening and project title
Say:
“Good morning. Our project is titled ‘Analysis of the Relationship Between Rainfall, Temperature and Maize Yield in Kenya.’ We chose agriculture because maize is an important food crop in Kenya, and its yield changes from year to year. We wanted to explore whether annual rainfall and temperature change alongside maize yield.”

Introduce all group members, or say:
“Our project group includes Patrick Muasya, Rotich Kipyegon, Kevin Kiplagat, Upao Mazibo, Hailu Samuel Dabaly, and Hope Wanjiru.”

2. Background and problem
Say:
“Maize yield can be affected by weather, but weather is only one influence. Yield may also depend on farming methods, soil, seed varieties, pests, and other inputs. Our problem was to examine the historical data and see what patterns exist between climate and maize yield in Kenya.”

Explain the project’s boundary:
“We focused on national annual data for maize yield, rainfall, and temperature. We are looking for patterns and associations, not claiming that climate alone determines yield.”

3. Research questions
Present these in everyday language:
- How did maize yield change over the years?
- Do years with more or less rainfall tend to have different maize yields?
- Do warmer or cooler years tend to have different maize yields?
- How well do rainfall and temperature together account for differences in maize yield?

4. Phase 1: planning the work
Say:
“In Phase 1, we selected the agriculture domain, defined the problem, set our research questions, and identified public secondary data sources. We planned to use FAOSTAT for maize data, the World Bank Climate Change Knowledge Portal for rainfall and temperature, and KNBS Economic Survey reports for national context.”

Briefly explain “secondary data”:
“Secondary data means information already collected and published by an organization. We did not conduct interviews or surveys for this project.”

Transition:
“After planning the study, Phase 2 put that plan into practice: we assembled the available records, checked and joined them, then explored them in R.”

SPEAKER 2 — PHASE 2 DATA, ANALYSIS, AND CONCLUSION

5. Data sources and period
Say:
“We matched the agricultural and climate files using year. The common period was 2010 through 2024, inclusive, so the final dataset contains 15 annual observations.”

Explain the variables and units:
- Year: the calendar year used to match records.
- Rainfall: annual total, measured in millimetres (mm).
- Temperature: annual mean, measured in degrees Celsius (°C).
- Maize yield: maize produced per harvested area, measured in tonnes per hectare (t/ha).
- Harvested area: included as context, measured in million hectares.

Emphasize:
“These are national annual measurements. They do not show the conditions for every county, farm, or growing season.”

6. Phase 2: cleaning and preparation
Say:
“We standardized the column names and data types, converted the climate series into year-and-value records, and joined the files by year. When a climate source had more than one record within a year, the records were averaged to create an annual value. We then sorted the combined data by year and saved the cleaned dataset for analysis.”

Explain the quality checks:
“We checked the number of records and the year range, looked for missing values and duplicate years, and reviewed values that could be unusual, such as negative rainfall or temperatures outside a broad review range.”

Simple explanation of why we joined by year:
“Year was the common identifier available across the sources. Matching by year made it possible to compare climate and yield for the same year.”

7. Explain the figures
Use the report’s five figures in order. Point to the axes and describe what is visible.

Figure 1 — Maize yield over time
“Yield goes up and down rather than following a steady rise or fall. It ranges from 1.43 t/ha in 2016 to 1.82 t/ha in 2015. In 2024 it is 1.67 t/ha.”

Figure 2 — Annual rainfall over time
“Rainfall varies considerably. It is lowest in 2014, at about 627 mm, and highest in 2019, at about 1,146 mm. In 2024 rainfall is about 727 mm.”

Figure 3 — Annual mean temperature over time
“Annual mean temperatures vary within a narrower range, from 24.84 °C in 2018 to 25.55 °C in 2023. The graph shows year-to-year changes, not the conditions at each farm or during each growing season.”

Figure 4 — Rainfall and yield scatterplot
“Each dot represents one year. The points are spread out, with a slight downward direction overall. Some wetter years have lower yields, but yields also differ at similar rainfall levels. The plot alone does not tell us that the relationship is statistically reliable.”

Figure 5 — Temperature and yield scatterplot
“Each dot again represents one year. The points show a weak downward tendency and are widely spread. Different yields occur at similar temperatures. We should not call the relationship significant just from looking at the graph.”

8. Explain the statistical results
Correlations:
“The rainfall and yield correlation is about negative 0.246, with a p-value of 0.3769. The temperature and yield correlation is about negative 0.273, with a p-value of 0.3256. Both estimated directions are negative, and both associations are weak in this sample.”

Plain-language meaning:
“A correlation describes how two variables move together in the data. A negative value means that higher values of one variable tended to occur alongside lower values of the other in this sample. The p-values are not small, so this analysis does not provide strong statistical evidence of a linear association. That does not prove there is no relationship; there are only 15 observations.”

Multiple regression:
“We also fitted a multiple regression model, which considers rainfall and temperature together when estimating yield. The rainfall coefficient is −0.0001578 t/ha per additional millimetre, with p = 0.478. The temperature coefficient is −0.154986 t/ha per additional degree Celsius, with p = 0.412. These estimates describe associations while holding the other included climate measure constant; they are not cause-and-effect estimates.”

R-squared:
“The model R-squared is 0.1139, or about 11.4%. In this sample, rainfall and temperature together account for about 11.4% of the observed differences in yield. That is a limited share. The overall model p-value is 0.4841, so the results do not show strong predictive performance in this small dataset.”

9. Limitations
Say:
“Our main limitation is the small dataset: 15 annual national observations. National averages hide local differences. Annual values also hide seasonal timing, dry spells, and extreme conditions during important growth stages. The model leaves out other influences on yield, including soils, seeds, pests, farming methods, fertilizer, irrigation, and other inputs. For these reasons, our results show associations only and cannot prove that rainfall or temperature caused a change in maize yield.”

10. Conclusion
Say:
“To conclude, maize yield varied from year to year between 2010 and 2024. Rainfall and temperature each had weak negative correlations with yield in this sample. The two-variable regression explained a limited share of yield variation and did not show strong predictive performance. Our analysis is useful for describing the data, but it cannot establish cause and effect. A future study could use county-level or season-level data and include more agricultural factors.”

HANDOFF BETWEEN SPEAKERS
Speaker 1 closes with:
“That covers the question and plan we developed in Phase 1. I’ll hand over to [Speaker 2] to explain how we prepared and analyzed the data in Phase 2.”

Speaker 2 begins with:
“Thank you. I’ll now explain the data we used, how we prepared it, and what the results show.”

LIKELY QUESTIONS AND SUGGESTED ANSWERS

Q: Why did you use 2010–2024?
A: That was the common period represented in the matched files. It gives 15 consecutive annual records with the needed variables.

Q: Why match the data by year?
A: Year was the common time identifier in the agriculture and climate files, so it allowed us to pair each year’s climate values with that year’s maize yield.

Q: What does a negative correlation mean here?
A: In these observations, higher rainfall or temperature tended to occur alongside somewhat lower maize yield. The relationships are weak and the points are widely spread.

Q: Are the relationships statistically significant?
A: The p-values are 0.3769 for rainfall and 0.3256 for temperature. They do not provide strong statistical evidence of a linear association in this small sample. We should not infer significance from the plots alone.

Q: Does the analysis prove that climate caused lower yields?
A: No. This is observational national annual data, and important factors are not included. It shows associations, not causation.

Q: What does R-squared of 0.1139 mean?
A: The model accounts for about 11.4% of the observed variation in yield in this dataset. Most of the variation is not accounted for by these two predictors in this model.

Q: What would improve the project?
A: More observations, county-level and season-level measurements, and variables such as soil, seed varieties, pests, fertilizer, irrigation, and farming practices could give a fuller picture.

PRESENTATION TIPS
- Keep the two speakers close to the same speaking time; rehearse once with a timer.
- Do not read every paragraph word for word. Use the quoted text as a guide and explain the figures in your own words.
- When showing a graph, point out what each axis represents before describing the pattern.
- Say “associated with,” “tended to occur with,” or “in this sample.” Avoid saying rainfall or temperature “caused” the yield changes.
- Use the exact units: rainfall in mm, temperature in °C, yield in t/ha, and harvested area in million ha.
- If asked about a p-value, explain that it is part of assessing statistical evidence; it is not the probability that the research question is true.
- End by returning to the main message: the project describes patterns in 15 national annual observations and cannot establish causation.
